Calling Bell & Talking Clock — Start With One Unit

One-unit calling bell and talking clock. 220 V AC, local-only. ESP-12F + PCF8563 + PAM8403 + 433 MHz EV1527. Sample ≈₹1,738; production target ₹540 at five units.

Bird's Eye View

Press the outdoor remote → chime. On the hour → spoken time, except during bedtime. Set time and upload chimes from a phone on the same Wi-Fi. The coin cell keeps the clock through power cuts.

Signal flow: button → 433 MHz receiver → ESP-12F → 1-bit audio on GPIO3 → RC filter → PAM8403 → speaker. The phone only configures the device over local Wi-Fi. Mains 220 V AC becomes 5 V in the isolated supply; a 3.3 V rail feeds the brain, clock, and receiver.

How the Two Basic Functions Are Achieved

Both functions share one audio path: file in flash → GPIO3 → RC filter → PAM8403 → speaker. Only the trigger differs.

1. Play any custom sound as the calling bell

  1. Convert your audio to 8 kHz / 8-bit mono with the Step 6 converter; upload it from the phone page into LittleFS (about 3 MB free for chimes).
  2. EV1527 code arrives on GPIO14; firmware matches the learned 24-bit ID and detaches that interrupt during playback.
  3. Bits stream on GPIO3 → 1 kΩ/0.1 µF filter → PAM8403 → speaker. HTTP is deferred; the interrupt is reattached afterwards.

2. Speak the clock every hour, with a bedtime

  1. Set date/time once from the phone browser into the PCF8563; the CR2032 keeps it through power cuts. No internet time source.
  2. Every minute the firmware reads the RTC. At HH:00, outside the quiet-hours window set in the web page (e.g. 22:00–06:00, across midnight), it plays stitched spoken pieces from LittleFS (~200 KB, 8 kHz 8-bit mono).
  3. Same audio path as the chime; watchdog guards the 24/7 loop. Inside the quiet window it stays silent.

Step 4 — Pre-Power-On Verification & First Power

Run every low-voltage check with the mains unplugged and the factory USB-UART cable disconnected. A multimeter in continuity/beep mode and DC volts mode is sufficient. Mains-side checks are electrician-only. Do not proceed past a failed row.

Step 5 — Core Firmware MVP

Lean C/C++ on the Arduino ESP8266 core or ESP8266 RTOS SDK, with a LittleFS partition on the 4 MB flash. Deliver complete, untruncated code covering:

  • PCF8563 timekeeping over I2C on GPIO4/5 — set, read, and maintain date/time locally.
  • EV1527 433 MHz decoding from the GPIO14 hardware interrupt — learn and match the 24-bit transmitter ID.
  • 1-bit Delta-Sigma LittleFS audio playback on GPIO3 — the RC filter feeds the PAM8403.
  • Real-time stability — detach the GPIO14 interrupt during playback and re-attach immediately after, pause/defer HTTP handling while audio plays, and use the watchdog for 24/7 uptime.

No audio drive during the factory UART flash; OTA only afterwards. A chime must play cleanly while the GPIO14 interrupt and web traffic are active.

Step 6 — Local Web Portal & Audio Pipeline

Serve a lightweight Vanilla HTML/JS portal directly from LittleFS with no external CDN links. It must let the user:

  • Set/sync the RTC date and time from the phone browser clock.
  • Upload and delete custom calling-bell audio files.
  • Pair/learn a 433 MHz EV1527 24-bit transmitter button ID.
  • Configure hourly/scheduled talking-clock announcements and quiet hours.
  • Trigger local OTA firmware updates.

Deliver a batch conversion command that produces talking-clock clips at 8 kHz 8-bit mono (about 200 KB total), preserving roughly 3 MB of flash for custom chimes and UI assets. Keep the portal deferred during audio playback as required in Step 5.
