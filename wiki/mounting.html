Radar Placement

A ceiling fan sitting directly under the radar is the single worst-case mounting. Here is why, and the placement that removes it.

Jump to: The ceiling fan geometry Why fans trigger radar Where to put it instead 10-minute field test
Failure Mode to Test

A spinning ceiling fan can trigger a mmWave radar looking straight down. In rooms with high ceilings and heavy 3-blade or 4-blade fans, mounting the radar in the ceiling rose can create false presence and unstable automation. Do not use the ceiling rose above a fan unless the assembled installation has been tested and the fan is outside the configured detection gates.

The Ceiling Rose Geometry Problem

Here is the cross-section of a standard ceiling rose installation. The radar module looks straight down from the ceiling slab. The fan motor, downrod, and spinning pitched blades are directly in its path of maximum sensitivity, only 200–400 mm away.

Why Fans Trigger mmWave Radar

The false positive, spinning
What the radar sees when nobody is in the room. Blades, wobble and the metal canopy all move radially for a few millimetres at a time. That is the same speed range as a breathing chest, which is why a fan overhead reads as a person sitting still.

First-order Doppler theory suggests that because fan blades rotate perpendicular to the line of sight, radial velocity should be zero. In reality, four physical factors cause intense Doppler return:

A dual-blade or four-blade Indian ceiling fan at 300 RPM puts every one of these effects in front of the sensor:

  • Aerodynamic Blade Pitch: Real fan blades are aerofoils angled at 10° to 14°. As each blade sweeps past the sensor, its leading and trailing edges approach and recede at 10–20 Hz, creating periodic modulation.
  • Mechanical Wobble: Ceiling fans wobble by 1–3 mm at 150–300 RPM. This yields a radial velocity of 0.01–0.1 m/s — matching human breathing chest motion.
  • Multipath Reflection: The metal motor canopy and ceiling hook bounce RF waves repeatedly, creating phantom Doppler targets.
  • Turbulent Airflow: High-speed downdrafts flutter nearby curtains, calendars, and lightweight room decor.

Where to put it instead

Separate the radar from the fan. Put it in a small ABS enclosure at 2.2 m on a side wall, looking across the room at an oblique downward angle. The Tinxy plate stays in the switchboard where the mains wiring already terminates — the sensor never goes near mains.

Recommended Mounting · Side Wall at 2.2 m
Real-World Wall Mounting: Mounted on the side wall at 2.2 m, the radar beam illuminates the entire floor plan. The ceiling fan is placed behind and well above the sensor's field of view, keeping near-field gates completely free of mechanical vibration.

Field Verification Protocol (10-Minute Test)

  1. Bench Setup: Connect the HLK-LD2410S to a USB-UART adapter (3.3 V, GND, TX, RX). Open the official HLKRadarTool app or a serial terminal (115,200 baud).
  2. Mount Temporarily: Use double-sided tape to attach the radar to the proposed wall position at 2.2 m.
  3. Run Fan at Maximum: Turn the ceiling fan to its highest speed setting with no people in the room. Observe the raw energy readings across Gates 0 through 8.
  4. Set Gate Cutoffs: Increase the radar_min_range gate until the fan energy drops below threshold (typically Gate 1 or 2 at 1.4 m).
  5. Verify Seated Presence: Sit still on the sofa or chair for 3 minutes. Confirm that the static detection flag remains solidly active.
  6. Verify the Vacancy Window: Leave the room and confirm the sensor drops out within the configured no-one duration before the fan is allowed to switch off.
